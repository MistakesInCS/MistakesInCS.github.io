## Introduction

Have you ever noticed that the word "first" is in alphabetical order? Probably, but you probably also didn't care about it. I, on the other hand, care about stupid things, so I wanted to know how "in order" English is in general. To do this, I used both gradient descent and a genetic algorithm to optimize the problem, both written from scratch in C#. Using gradient descent required transforming the problem from a discrete one into a continuous one. At the end, I compare the distributions of orders generated by these methods, and then compare them to a few "almost hand made" orders generated by metrics rather than optimization methods. 

Of course, most words aren't fully ordered, but ending the problem there would be underwhelming. Therefore, I needed a way to compare partial orders. Instead of using something sensical like the number of inversions, I decided to generate a score for each word by comparing only adjacent letters. For each letter in a word, compare it to the next letter; if the two letters (the 2-gram) are in order, then add one to the score; if the two letters are not in order, subtract one; if they are the same letter, do nothing. By this metric, "first" has a score of 5, since every letter comes before the letter after it, but a word like "and" has a score of 0, since a comes before n, but n comes after d. 

At first, I didn't know what to expect, since there are common patterns like consonant-vowel-consonant and -ing endings. If the letters in words were uniformly sampled from the alphabet, you would expect the average score to tend to 0, but letters are not uniformly sampled; "e" is much more common than "q," for example. I could really see any outcome being viable.

I needed a ton of English words. I used [this github](https://github.com/dwyl/english-words) with 400k words. I excluded words that were not exclusively English letters, like "A.D." After some kinks, the average score of a word in the dataset was... -0.000375. Strangely close to 0! There are 2930714 valid 2-grams in the dataset, and the total score was -128. For almost every 2-gram that was out of order, there was one that was in order. 

Now it was time to actually try and find a better ordering. It would be impractical to simply randomly permute the letters and then completely re-examine the words. We can use the fact that the words being examined are constant in order to make this more efficient.

Let the score function be $$S(\alpha)$$, where $$\alpha$$ is the ordering. The goal is then $$\operatorname{argmax}_\limits{\alpha}$$ $$S(\alpha)$$, where $$S(\alpha)$$ $$= \sum_{w \in W} s(w)$$, $$W$$ is the set of words $$w$$, and $$s(w)$$ is the score of word $$w$$. $$s(w) = \sum_i s(w_i, w_{i+1})$$, where $$w_i$$ is the character in the i-th position, and $$s$$ is +1 when $$w_i$$ precedes $$w_{i+1}$$ in the ordering, 0 if they are the same, and -1 otherwise. Therefore, $$S(\alpha) = \sum_{w_i} s(w_i, w_{i+1})$$, or that the score of the alphabet is the sum of the scores of all the 2-grams it contains. Note that letters do not cross word boundaries when considering the next letter; the last letter of a word simply does not have a next letter, even if my butchery of notation implies it.

Next, count every 2-gram in the text. Then, make a matrix $$\mathbf{M}$$ whose rows and columns are indexed by the letters of the alphabet. Put the count of each 2-gram of the form AB into the corresponding element $$m_{a,b}$$. The sum of every element in this matrix is the total number of 2-grams in the dataset. This sum is $$sum = \sum_{a,b \in \alpha} m_{a,b}$$, or in other words, the sum of the count of each 2-gram. Expanding, $$m_{a,b} = \sum_{w_i} c_{a,b}(w_i, w_{i+1})$$, where $$c_{a,b} = 1$$ if $$w_i = a$$ and $$w_{i+1} = b$$, and 0 otherwise. Rewriting the sum, $$sum_\mathbf{M} = \sum_{a,b \in \alpha} \sum_{w_i} c_{a,b}(w_i, w_{i+1}) = \sum_{w_i} \sum_{a,b \in \alpha} c_{a,b}(w_i, w_{i+1})$$.

Create a new matrix, $$\mathbf{S}$$, where each element $$s_{a,b} = s(a,b)m_{a,b}$$, or that each element in $$\mathbf{S}$$ is the corresponding element in $$\mathbf{M}$$, but positive if a precedes b, negative if b precedes a, and 0 if they are the same. The sum of this matrix is then $$sum_\mathbf{S} = \sum_{w_i} \sum_{a,b \in \alpha} s(a,b) c_{a,b}(w_i, w_{i+1})$$. Note that, for a fixed $$w_i$$, $$\sum_{a,b \in \alpha} s(a,b) c_{a,b}(w_i, w_{i+1})$$ reduces to $$s(w_i, w_{i+1})$$, since $$c_{a,b} = 1$$ for exactly one pair of a and b, so $$sum_S = \sum_{w_i} s(w_i, w_{i+1}) = S(\alpha)$$. This is a very roundabout way of saying that the new elements sum the total score of each 2-gram instead of simply counting them, and the sum of the matrix is therefore the score of the alphabet.

Notice that the elements of $$\mathbf{S}$$ are negative below the diagonal. This is not a coincidence! The only negative elements in any row are going to be in columns corresponding to the letters that precede it. Since the letters are mirrored on the top and bottom, every element before the element along the diagonal will correspond to a 2-gram with a negative score, so those are the negative elements. Note that this is true for every ordering! So this gives a compact way to calculate the score of an ordering: permute the rows and columns of the 2-gram matrix $$\mathbf{S}$$ to generate the new ordering, then take the Frobenius product with some sign matrix Sign, where the elements along the diagonal are 0, above the diagonal are 1, and below the diagonal are -1. This is much better than checking almost 3 million 2-grams individually!

## Finding a Better Order

Now to actually find a better order. It's obviously unfeasible to check all $$26!$$ orders, so I had to use optimization methods.

### Gradient Descent

At first, I wanted to use gradient descent, which might sound like a weird choice, since this is a decidedly continuous problem. And you would be right. But I wanted to figure out a way to pose this problem in a continuous way and see how well a continuous optimization method would work. 

To make the problem continuous, I used permutation matrices. Taking a permutation matrix $$\mathbf{P}$$, taking $$\mathbf{PSP}^T = \mathbf{S}'$$ results in a matrix $$S'$$ with the rows and columns permuted in the same way, so if A and B are swapped in the ordering, it will result in swapping the first and second rows and columns. The final score for the ordering would be

$$
S(\mathbf{P}, \mathbf{S}) = \langle \mathbf{P}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{P^T}, \mathbf{\mathrm{Sign}} \rangle_F
$$

Instead of being smart and using someone else's code to do this for me, I decided to implement a gradient descent algorithm myself in C#. This was very enlightening, but also gave me lots of problems, mainly because I had a lot of trouble taking the derivative. 

Originally, I wanted to constrain the values using some kind of activation function, like tanh, but this made everything way more complicated, and I decided to use penalties on the score instead. The penalties were designed to make the elements of the learned permutation matrix tend towards 1 or 0, and make the sum of each row and column tend towards 1. This was its own problem, since my gradients ended up either exploding and making the values ridiculous, or simply not moving them at all. I wanted to solve the problem by adding a sliding learning rate that values the score most in early iterations, but then values the penalties at the end, but everything was still going pretty poorly. Eventually I settled on using a fixed length for each learning step, decaying over time, and then turning the invalid learned permutation matrix into a learned one by taking the maximum element, setting it to 1, and repeating, disregarding rows and columns that already had a 1 defined. 

Ultimately, the loss to be optimized is

$$
l = -S(\mathbf{P}, \mathbf{S}) + \sum_{p \in P} f(p) + g(\mathrm{rowSum}_p) + g(\mathrm{colSum}_p),
$$

where

$$
S(\mathbf{P}, \mathbf{S}) = \langle \mathbf{P}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{P^T}, \mathbf{\mathrm{Sign}} \rangle_F, 
$$

$$
f(x) = x^2(x-1)^2,
$$

$$
g(x) = (x-1)^2,
$$

and rowSum and colSum are the sums of the elements of the respective rows and columns that p is in.

So the gradient for each iteration becomes 

$$
-\nabla l_{a,b} = \nabla_P S(\mathbf{P}, \mathbf{S})_{a,b} - (f'(p) + g'(\text{rowSum}) + g'(\text{colSum})),
$$

where 

$$
\nabla_P S(\mathbf{P}, \mathbf{S}) = \mathbf{S}^T \mathbf{PA} + \mathbf{SPA}^T,
$$

$$
f'(x) = 4x^3 - 6x^2 +2x,
$$

and

$$
g'(x) = 2(x-1).
$$

Full disclosure, that gradient for the score was originally generated by Gemini. I tried working it out by hand, several times, and got bad results each time. Gemini pulled out a strange but useful identity, where the total differential $$df = \langle \nabla f(\mathbf{P}), d\mathbf{P} \rangle_F = \operatorname{tr} (f(\mathbf{P}^T d\mathbf{P}) $$. I never would have thought of this on my own, and it's a much more consice proof than trying to do it manually and elementwise like I was originally doing. 

The gradient is a little more complicated, but note that since each element of the learned matrix is independent of each other (unlike if I had used tanh), most of the terms go to zero. In particular, all the other elements of the score gradient go to zero, as well as all the other penalties for different elements and different rows and columns.

I added several different factors to the gradient, however, to try and better fine-tune how it progressed. In particular, I multiply the score derivative by the ratio of how many iterations remain to the number of total iterations, and I muliply the penalty derivative by 100 and some exponential that increases as the number of remaining iterations decreases. 

Additionally, as I stated before, the gradient's length is normalized by dividing it by its 2-norm. This is then multiplied by the learning rate, to bring it to a constant length, and then multiplied by .99 to the power of the number of the current iteration, to add decay.

Finally, the permutation matrix is made valid using the method I described previously.

I start by randomly permuting the valid permutation matrix, and then repeatedly calculating the gradient of the loss and subtracting it from that permutation matrix. I'm also using momentum for one part, so technically it's being added to the momentum matrix there.

Overall, gradient descent performed... okay. Most of the time, the resulting score of the learned permutation is positive, which is good, but it's still upsetting that it can generate permutations with negative scores. Oddly enough, gradient descent with momentum = 0.9 performed worse than gradient descent without momentum, and with momentum = 0.3 it performed really well. The results will be discussed in more detail below.

### A Genetic Algorithm

I had originally given up on gradient descent since it originally wasn't working at all, so I decided to move on to a different method designed specifically for discrete optimization problems. (I would return to GD later, and with enough tweaking and fixing I eventually got it to work.) I opted for a genetic algorithm instead of simulated annealing.

The issue with a genetic algorithm is that it is not immediately obvious what the "genes" to inherit are or how to implement such an idea. If you say, for instance, that the child ordering should inherit some quality like "A comes before B," how should that be approached to prevent the child from inheriting the contradictory quality "B comes before A?" I developed two approaches and chose the simpler. 

In both approaches, I model each ordering as a graph of 26 nodes - one for each letter, and edges between the nodes - the number varying depending on the approach. In the more complex approach, I wanted to model the more general quality of "A comes before B" while still allowing letters to come in between them, so potential qualities are "A comes before B," "A comes before C," etc. Each such quality is represented by an edge connecting every letter to every other, so 26^2 edges. An edge leading from A to B implies that A comes before B in the ordering. Inheritance is simple: just choose an edge at random from a parent to give to the child. When building a new ordering, start with an empty graph, and add valid edges until the ordering is complete. An edge is valid if it does not create a cycle on the graph; there should be a path leading from the first letter to the last letter, reaching every letter along the way.

The reason I ultimately did not choose this method was because it would require maintaining an absurd number of edges and maintaining the trasitive property would be more difficult. Each letter of an ordering would require keeping track of every other letter's relative order, which would be $$26^2 = 676$$ edges. Checking if a potential order is valid and adding a new order would also require additional checks. I figured I would use something simpler that would probably run faster.

The simple approach entails only keeping edges between letters that directly follow or precede each other; this requires only 26 edges per ordering (technically, I give each letter two edges: one for the previous letter and one for the next, bringing the total to 52). This loses the ability to represent the higher level quality of "A comes before B at some point," but I decided that the simplicity and efficiency gains would outweigh that, and individual orderings would be more likely to survive if they captured such ideas - if A coming at some point before B is important to the quality of the ordering, then a descendant that inverts that ordering would perform worse.

In my implementation, I actually add two additional nodes to each ordering: a START and END node representing the start and end of the ordering, respectively. As mentioned, I give each node two edges that denote the previous and next letter. At the start, every node's edges point to itself. As edges are defined, they form incomplete suborders, e.g. B -> C -> D. 

To efficiently choose edges to define, I maintain two sets of letters: one of letters lacking a defined next edge (a "head" representing the end of a suborder), and one of letters lacking a defined previous next edge (a "tail" representing the beginning of a suborder). Note that, when a new order is added, both a head and a tail are removed from the set; there will always be an equal number of elements in each set. 

As orders are added, I point the next edge of the last element of a suborder to the first element, and the previous edge of the first element of a suborder to the last element, which allows me to quickly check for invalid orders. Letters that are not the first or last in a suborder will necessarily have defined next and previous edges, so they will not be in the head set nor the tail set, so they will not be considered for new orders at all. Letters at the end can therefore only have potential orders made with letters at an opposite end, but note that, for a head, the only invalid tail to be chosen would be the tail that is in its own suborder, and vice versa, so maintaining these elements in the pointers of heads and tails is an efficient way to check if adding an order would create a cycle.

As long as any letters have undefined edges (i.e. the head or tail set is not empty), I choose a head at random and try to choose a valid tail to assign as the next letter. For randomly selecting an order, simply choosing a random tail and checking if it would create a cycle is enough. To satisfy the genetic component of the algorithm, however, it is necessary to inherit at least some orders from parent orderings. I allow for a variable number of parents, so I simply choose one at random, pick the letter that follows the chosen head, and check if that is a valid order to add. If that is not a valid order, I try again. There is a small mutation rate that ignores the genes of the parents and simply assigns a random valid order; this adds an explorative element and also prevents the program from locking up from running out of valid orders from parents. 

When a head is assigned a next element, four pointers are updated: the chosen head's next edge points to the chosen tail, the cohsen tail's previous edge points to the chosen head, the head of the new suborder's next edge points to the new tail (via the chosen head's previous next edge), and the tail of the new suborder's previous edge points to the new head (via the chosen tail's previous previous edge). 

Hopefully this diagram explains it in more detail. Note that the previous edges are ommitted, since they are just the reverse of the next edge.

In this example scenario, the head set contains C, E, and START, and the tail set contains A, D, and F. If I choose a random head to try to assign, say C, and then try to assign the tail A, I would see that the next edge of C already points to A, so it is an invalid order. If I then take C and try to assign D, I would see that that is a valid order, so C's next edge would point to D, D's previous edge would point to C, E's next edge would point to A, and A's previous edge would point to E:

After this, C is removed from the head set, and D is removed from the tail set.

There is another validity check for edges that I didn't mention before: it is important that suborders do not start with START and end with END without having every letter included, otherwise there is no way to add those extra letters back into the middle of the order.

Once there are no more heads to assign, the order is complete. 

Scoring each of these orders is actually really simple. As before, the score of an ordering is the Frobenius product of the corresponding permutation of the matrix of 2-gram scores and that special sign matrix. Treat an ordering as a pairing of letter and index, so that it is a sequence of pairs like (Z, 0), (X, 1), etc. Note that the element (0,1) of the permuted score matrix would correspond, in that example, to the 2-gram pair (Z, X), which does not change its numeric value through the permutation process. As such, if I treat the indices of the score matrix as letters instead of numbers, it is still valid to call that element (Z, X). All that needs to be done, then, is to decide if the element should be positive or negative based on the ordering, which is as simple as the row index being less than the column index. Therefore, the code looks like

```{C#}

for(int row = 0; row < 26; row++){
    for(int col = 0; col < 26; col++){
        if(col < row)
            fitness -= scoreMatrix[orderList[row+1]-'a',orderList[col+1]-'a'];
        else
            fitness += scoreMatrix[orderList[row+1]-'a',orderList[col+1]-'a'];
    }
}

return fitness;

```

where orderList is the indexable order and fitness is the score of the order. Note that the indices to orderList are +1 since START is the 0th element. The returned character then has 'a' subtracted from it in order to get the numerical index in the normal alphabet, which is then used to index the scoreMatrix for the score of the corresponding 2-gram. The score is then added or subtracted from the sum depending on the position in the permuted matrix as a replacement for a direct Frobenius product.

The rest of the process for the genetic algorithm is pretty simple. First, tons of random orders are generated. Then, they are sorted by score, and the worst are culled (in my simulations, the lowest 80%). They are allowed to repopulate, with random numbers of parent orders interbreeding, until they reach the population cap. Then they are again scored and culled. This process repeats for some number of generations (in my simulations, 130), and progressively better and better orders are generated. Note that culling the worst prevents the best orders from being lost, so there can never be a worsening of best orders across generations. At the end, the very best order is the output.

The genetic algorithm takes a lot longer than the gradient descent algorithm, but produces much, much better results.

## Conclusion

This was a fun project to work on, even if it took me several months of dragging my feet to actually complete it, and then a few weeks of reading Dune and Dune Messiah instead of writing the blog post. I am mostly surprised by how consistently the genetic algorithm put certain letters first; maybe this is a side-effect of genes being represented by simple (a,b) orders, instead of the more complicated (a < b) pairs that imply a more distant and more general order causing some pairs, like (Start, m) to be highly valued and then resulting in quick plateaus. The gradient descent methods seemed to be more uniform in their orderings, but were also worse, and it seems like the more uniform the letter positions, the worse the distribution performed in general. 

Gradient descent was a major hassle, and it felt like it would never work, until suddenly it did. And then, simply adding a little bit of momentum caused it to perform way better. Maybe the smaller value of momentum worked better since the learning rate is so small, or the number of iterations is low, or the overall loss surface is too chaotic to perform well with momentum - too much momentum, and large minima might be skipped over, since they are so small. 

The genetic algorithm worked spectacularly with minimal bugs out of the box. This is probably due to its relative simplicity, or, in other words, the fact that I don't have to calculate derivatives myself, and that I don't have to mess with the algorithm too much to get it to work with the problem. One issue is that It plateus super commonly at the end of each training cycle, with the entire population ending up as clones of the best performing order. I upped the mutation rate, and it didn't really change anything. I think a better implementation might involve limiting the number of duplicate individuals, but really I think that just changes how resources are spent in a not-necessarily-beneficial way. If you imagine the loss surface and each individual in the population as a point on that surface, you can then imagine children as adjacent points to their parents, and then draw a line between them. As each generation progresses, the paths branch with multiple children. Enforcing a copy limit simply spreads out individuals to be more broad, creating more branches from different nodes - which is the purpose, of course; however, this may not be optimal, as many children may explore poor sections of the surface. It may be better, later on, to hyper-optimize a few great minima with lots of children each, rather than half-optimize lots of different minima with a few children each. In this way, the genetic algorithm automatically implements an exploration-exploitation trade-off: explore early with lots of genetic paths, then exploit later as only a few genetic paths remain.

For a part 2, using more compute than a thinkpad would likely be key.
